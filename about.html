To navigate this website, you can first read through this explanation of my work, then through the Life, Death and Legacy tabs to see how gender affected agents in life, death, and legacy, and then finally the Transparency tab to see where I get my information.

Her work in the digital humanities and digital histories has seen her present twice previously at the Canadian Society for the Digital Humanities at FHSS Congress, with her work focusing on using digital methods to reveal hidden histories and identities of those in the past.

Who Were the S.O.E. Agents?

The Special Operations Executive (S.O.E.) was a civilian branch of the British Army during the Second World War, tasked with espionage behind enemy lines in the Asian and European theatres. Their agents ranged from housewives to hardened soldiers, with gender an integral difference within their experiences.

There is no exact estimate of how many men were deployed as field agents with the S.O.E., but my research shows that 45 women were. This can be debated, based on differing views of what counts as an S.O.E. agent, but my criteria is an agent who has been both recruited and has documentation proving their involvement, and that they were trained by the S.O.E.

The S.O.E. agents involved in this research have been divided into two parts:

For understanding the correlations between gender and life, there were 31 female agents who survived the war, who I then matched with 31 agents who were deployed alongside them, and survived.

14 of the female agents died during the war, who I then matched with 14 male agents who were deployed with them and died. This way, agents' experiences can be tracked alongside each other to see how gender varied from the moment of deployment.

Worth noting is that this project does not represent all male agents, but a sample. All percentages and discussions of gender should be understood as simply a snapshot, and does not claim to encompass all agents in all areas.

What's Missing?

The varying identities of S.O.E. agents create a unique area of analysis for gender history, and how masculinity and femininity separately affected these agents in recruitment, deployment, capture, execution, and survival, which has rarely been studied.

This is for a few reasons. Firstly, agents' files are difficult to access, as few of their files have been digitized, requiring researchers to have access to the British National Archives in Kew, London. As a student based in Canada, this was obviously not possible, forcing me to turn to alternative sources, such as official histories. However, as historian Mark Seaman explores, there are many issues with official histories as well, from financial and authorial bias to veterans suing over unfaithful retelling, and, returning to my earlier point about lack of access to the British National Archives, a difficulty for the public to fact-check what they read. 1

To return to concerns with the British National Archives, lack of access is a recognized issue in the ethics of archival work, as by not digitizing works, keeping them behind a paywall, and their location within institutions severely limits who can have access to information, which is not to say that digitization in itself is the answer. Digitization can remove the physicality of resources and also contain biases by those who digitize them, choosing what gets digitized and what does not, repeating the same accessibility issues with physical archives.2 My work, while in no way able to tackle these larger archival access issues, tries in its own way to allow the public access to my research. The Transparency tab contains a list of every agent and every resource I used to get information on them, allowing people to go through and see agents' information for themselves. For my code, all of my code is available alongside the visualizations they create, allowing people to see where the information is coming from.

Secondly, there have just been too few attempts at understanding gender in the lives of S.O.E. agents, with some exceptions.

Historian Juliette Pattinson has done extensive research on gender and S.O.E. agents in France, which has been a fantastic aid to me, alongside Sylvie Pomiès-Maréchal, whose work explores the sensationalization of female agents following the war, and the effect the media has had on agents' legacies.3 However, as beneficial as these two works are, they show just how limited the field of gendered S.O.E. analysis is to anyone who wants to understand gender in different areas in the world, or the general understanding of how gender affected an agent. Similarly, there have been no digital humanities projects done on S.O.E. agents, and few in general that focus on gender history. Projects such as WarVictimSampo and Project 44 show how beneficial digital history projects can be in understanding historical events and representing historical groups, and I hope that my website can live among them as another digital resource for those interested in the S.O.E., in the Second World War or in Gender history.

As such, I wanted to fill the gaps in this research, going beyond Pattinson’s exploration of gender performance in France and Pomiès-Maréchal’s discussions of legacies and finding new digital ways to explore these issues, resulting in the creation of this project, which set out to answer:

How did gender affect the lives, deaths, and legacies of S.O.E. agents?

My project, Understanding the Gendered S.O.E. Experience, answers this question through compiling a list of agents, programming the correlations, and analyzing how agents felt through their own accounts of their spywork.

Project Overview

To begin, I created a CSV of both Surviving and Dead agents using a variety of sources, such as M.R.D. Foot’s SOE in France: An Account of the Work of the British Special Operations Executive in France 1940-1944, and an Infiltration Log created by Plan Sussex. These sources allowed me to match female and male deployments and figure out a list of female agents. To fill out my categories of research, such as when an agent was born or the job they took, I used multiple sources online to find this information, from Award Recommendations to YouTube interviews, all of which are available under the transparency tab.

From there, I used Python Programming and Jupyter Notebook to take each CSV and break down the agents information into different categories, and then visualizations, such as maps of deployment and location of death, bar charts for the years of deployment and years of death, and mainly pie charts to show different categories such as jobs agents had in the field, locations of deaths, age, etc. This code is visible in the Life and Death tabs, allowing users to see my process in creating these visualizations.

Outside of the code, I wanted to understand agents’ experiences in their own words, rather than just numbers. As such, I read multiple books written by agents about their experiences, and listened to their interviews to see how they perceived their gender. Worth noting, however, is that these works were not created by agents to talk solely about gender, but instead their whole experiences as agents in the field. The information I have pulled from these books has been what I found relevant to their gender and work in the field, but agents could have thought differently about their gender than how I have framed their work. Unfortunately, as all S.O.E. agents have passed away, it is up to me to use these sources to the best of my ability, and I hope that I have represented them in ways that they would have liked to have been represented.

With this code created, I then used Visual Code Studio and GitHub to create a website to host my research, with the Life Tab containing information about agents who survived the war, the Death tab contains information about agents who died during the war, while Transparency contains the citation information for each agent involved.

The goal of Understanding the Gendered S.O.E. Experience is not just to teach about S.O.E agents, but to equally honour and recognize them regardless of gender. I hope that, once you have finished exploring this website, you take away new understandings of espionage, the Special Operations Executive agents, and how gender has affected people in countless ways throughout history.
