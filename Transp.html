Sources Used

Learn about the correlations between gender and life, death, and legacy of the Special Operations Executive Agents of the Second World War.

Information about S.O.E. agents is either closely guarded, limitedly available to the public, or widely available, but potentially exaggerated or false.

To begin, only 17 S.O.E. personnel files have been digitized, so if someone wants to access information about these agents, they would have to go to the British National Archives in London, which is a trip not many can make. Luckily, award recommendation files have been digitized to help piece together what these agents accomplished in the field, and you only need to make an account to have access to them.

So, why do some agents have their files accessible, while others don’t? Does this mean they weren’t actually agents?

The answer is a lot more complicated and may come down to a few different factors. It could be that some agents' information might still be kept back by the British Government for safety reasons, or that their files have been displaced or destroyed over the course of the war and the centuries afterward, but the most likely answer is that for agents who died more recently, their information likely hasn’t been made available to the public. As seen in the BNA files, there is typically a release date for information, and it simply hasn’t been enough time yet for the files to be opened.

The next step, then, is to turn to public sources, which can be dangerous. Some information available in movies or books is not accurate, as historians Mark Seaman and Sylvie Pomiès-Maréchal explore. Female agents, since the moment the public has learned of their existence, have been highly sensationalized, leading to lots of media coverage about them, but this coverage is not always accurate.1 Mark Seaman, who has explored different official S.O.E. histories, has found authorial and financial factors affecting what is actually told.2

As such, I have been careful to try and find sources that are accurate, but this cannot always be the case. As such, I have included every website and resource I used to ensure that, while other histories might not be transparent, mine can be.

To begin, I have a list of general sources consulted, and then a list of individual sources per agent included in the life and death categories.

GENERAL:

Infiltrations into France Log:

https://www.plan-sussex-1944.net/en/pdf/infiltrations_into_france.pdf

A compiled list of agent infiltrations into, and out of, France, to track the time agents spent in the field, as well as their fates.

M.R.D. Foot’s SOE in France: An Account of the Work of the British Special Operations Executive in France 1940-1944

https://www.generalstaff.org/WW2/Hist_UK/SOE-in-France_1940-44.pdf

Perhaps the most famous work done on the history of France by the S.O.E.’s official historian. This book covers only French agents; however, considering most female agents were deployed in France (and so, then, were the male agents of my datasets), it served as a good base. However, as I mentioned above with scholar Mark Seaman, we must be careful of accepting S.O.E. histories as immediately true, and M.R.D. Foot, despite his title of official historian, is no exception. Originally, on the release of this work, agents were so displeased with it that Foot was sued and many changes were made.3 In fact, Maurice Buckmaster provided 35 pages worth of feedback!4 As such, the history was changed, making it difficult to know how accurate it is on two fronts: the first in Foot's own decisions in creating the history, and the second in whatever changes were made following its publication.

Liane Jones’ A Lonely Courage

This work utilizes interviews with female S.O.E. agents following the war to piece together their stories. While it does provide a narrative account, I did not use these, as I was rather more interested in the interviews Jones did with the S.O.E. agents, and only used those parts in my work.

Juliette Pattinson’s Behind Enemy Lines: Gender, Passing and the Special Operations Executive in the Second World War

This work is perhaps the best resource for understanding gender in S.O.E.’s lives, and was consulted both for facts about agents, but also to see if my correlations tracked what her research had shown.
