Legacy

While it is important to understand how gender impacted agents in life and death, it’s also important to see how gender impacted agents after the war, in how they are remembered.

To do so, we’ll look at two categories: Film & Written Works and Awards.

Films & Written Works



Since the beginning of the S.O.E., female agents have caught the attention of the public eye, as explored by Sylvie Pomiès-Maréchal. Many female agents were highly discussed with countless news articles produced about their work, especially for female agents who did not survive the war, such as Violette Szabo. As Pomes-Marchel explains, “‘when women are actively involved in ways that challenge societal codes about femininity, a voyeuristic fascination ensues”, and many sought to profit off of such fascination, with many news articles turning to sensationalism to build up ideas of female agents.1 This sentiment is shared by M.R.D. Foot, one of the official S.O.E. historians, who claimed that, with lack of information, “ghastly imputations about what happened to these girls have been freely inserted by sensation mongers”.2 This is true even today, with headlines like “British girl who spied on Nazis used beauty to woo Germans - and talked her way out of execution”, and countless films and books produced about spies to varying levels of accuracy.3

As someone who has spent quite some time studying S.O.E. agents, I, too, have consumed S.O.E. films and books, many of which took fictionalized spins on these agents' stories to varying levels of success.

To begin, my scrutiny of these works does not come from the concept of these fictional accounts. I am well aware that S.O.E. agents can make for great stories, as my first introduction to these agents was through the media, and many works do not claim to be wholly accurate or actually represent historical events.

However, there is a difference between using the S.O.E. as a backdrop for an action block-buster, and telling a story about the S.O.E. To explain this distinction better, I offer two examples: the movie The Ministry of Ungentlemanly Warfare, and the novel The Black Swan of Paris.

The Ministry of Ungentlemanly Warfare, which came out in 2024, begins with the claim that it was “based on a true story”, and during the end credits, claims it is based on a novel of the same name.4 This immediately drew my ire, as they use real agents names, and yet not real agents stories. Related to this project on gender, these inaccuracies mainly lie in Marjorie Stewart, one of only two female characters in the film. Marjorie Stewart was a real woman, and in the film, she is an active S.O.E. agent in the field, whereas in real life, she worked behind the scenes as a secretary and in training.5 She was included in the film, according to the director, as she married the main character the movie was based on (in the book the film is based on, she is mentioned once in a sentence about said marriage), and because she could “stand in for all of the female spies who never got their due”, claiming that “If we didn’t put Marjorie in there, we’d be forgetting all of the women that were there and the work that she did specifically on all the different missions.”6 This, you might think, is a good message! Their characterization of Marjorie, however, makes this comment a joke. In the movie, she seduces Nazi officers, wearing outfits no woman would have worn in the 1940s, and in general, is not an homage to the many women who were part of the S.O.E., but a continuation of the oversexualization these agents face after death. This is not to say that female agents ought to be depicted as sexless individuals or to be infantilized and kept ‘pure’, as women did have sex lives and used their gender to help them in the field, such as going on dates with German officers, but they were not deployed in the field specifically for seduction.7 This is more than just a blockbuster using more explosions and action to make sales; this is the undervaluing and misrepresenting of women in the field by “sensation mongers” as M.R.D. Foot considers them, whose ideas of female spies rest in the seductive femme fatale, rather than the truth they claim for this film.8 The Ministry of Ungentlemanly Warfare is not alone in this issue, as explored by historian Sylvie Pomiès-Maréchal. In her discussion of another film, Female Agents, is riddled with inaccuracies, including a female agent whose speciality is “seduction”, and one female resistance member, Denise Vernay, was so distraught by the film and its errors that creating such films were “worse than if they had done nothing”, a sentiment I agree with.9 It would have been better, in the case of the Ministry of Ungentlemanly Warfare, to have not included Marjorie’s character than to reduce female S.O.E. to sexual objects, and to reduce a real woman to such a character.

Evidently, I feel quite strongly about this film, so let us compare to a piece I feel like handles this sort of situation better: The Black Swan of Paris, a novel written by Karen Robards. To begin, it does not claim to be true, and the author states at the end of the novel that “its very possible that some factual errors have slipped in. If so, they are mine alone”.10 The S.O.E. agents in the novel are entirely fictionalized, being Max Bonet, aka Major Max Ryan, who evaded suspicion through being ‘unfit’ for labour through his disability, and Emmanuelle Rocheford, a French woman whose knowledge of French and connections within the country made her fit to be an agent. While it is certainly a drama, with a romance between the main female character, Genevra Rocheford and Max Ryan, with her cover being a celebrity singer to hide espionage and secret workings, it handles issues within the S.O.E. more tactfully. The forms of espionage, such as using cigarettes to smuggle messages, is real, S.O.E. agents in the field are kept to their separate circuits, the torture is fairly realistic, and while it does involve Genevra being forced to go to dinner with a Nazi officer, it is never framed as seduction, but something Genevra is deeply uncomfortable with and must do for the sake of survival for her and her circuit. Naturally, a novel and an action movie will have different ways to accomplish storytelling, but the difference between the Ministry of Ungentlemanly Warfare and the Black Swan of Paris shows how characterization and intent go a long way in creating a good action story while remaining historically accurate.

This issue of accuracy is one that is prevalent even within S.O.E. histories or works written by S.O.E. agents, as M.R.D. Foot claimed that many such books had made for “good thrillers, but bad history”.11 As well, works created were heavily influenced by publishers as explored by historian Mark Seaman, as he claims they wanted to sell books as “out and out adventure stories”, not historical retellings.12 Agents and officers, then, who wrote their accounts “were encouraged to let the ‘action’ dominate”, leading to works of varying levels of truth. 13

Agents, however, did not willingly accept overdramatization of their lives, and tried their hardest to ensure their legacies were accurate ones. Odette Sansom, who had a biography and movie created about her life, was well aware of how her story could be turned into a blockbuster. Speaking to the man who wrote her biography, Jerrard Tickell, about turning his book into a film, she stated “If you are fond of me as you say, don’t let the film be done by the Americans. They will make it a Hollywood story and my story is a Hollywood story enough — if you knew the end of it.” 14 Well before productions like the Ministry of Ungentlemanly Warfare, Odette was aware that people could blow her story out of proportion, which is not what she wanted. Jean Claude Guiet was well aware of the excitement surrounding agents, which he considered “James Bond Syndrome” that made his story of interest to people, and he also recognized that the media surrounding Violette Szabo, his courier, further made his stories of interest.15 Nancy Wake similarly had thoughts on this, as she was often embarrassed reading articles about herself, as “frequently they would give the reader the impression that I myself won every battle that was fought in the Auvergne”, and that “one article stated I had led my 8,000 men to victory. I had a mental picture of myself surging ahead (rather in the style of Joan of Arc) followed by the faithful 8,000.”16 Wake did not only have thoughts on her own legacy, but on other depictions of women as well. She recounts a film about an escape-route network where “the woman leader gave orders for a pilot to be shot simply because he had the name of a German girl in his address book. Perhaps he had been guilty of some dangerous indiscretion—or even worse— but to shoot him out of hand without first interrogating him would have been stupid” showing how, while some agents turned to inaccuracy to sell their works, others were highly critical of those trying to profit off of their legaciess with false stories.17

Outside of inaccurate legacies, media created by and about agents also had to handle the legacy of agents who were not able to tell their own stories. A common theme in S.O.E. agents who created works, whether writing memoirs or participating in the creation of films, was honouring their fellow agents. Odette Sansom, who received plenty of publicity through a biography written by Jerrard Tickell, and a film based on the biography, often found it difficult to relive these times of her life, but pushed on for her fellow female agents. “I [had to] take myself away from thinking it’s to do with me. It is to do with all the girls. I wanted it made for all of them. I must remove the emotional element.”18 Odette repeated this sentiment when her biography was turned into a film, as at the end, she states “It is to [the female S.O.E. agents] memory that this film has been made and I would like it to be a window through which may be seen those very gallant women with whom I had the honour to serve”.19 The same was for Phylis Latour, whose co-author stated that Phylis “would only talk about her life if the fourteen brave SOE women who died were acknowledged. She had huge respect for them and wanted people to know about their sacrifice far more than she wanted to talk about her own experiences.”20

Altogether, the media surrounding agents has affected their legacies, from oversensationalization leading the public to perceive female agents as seductive femme fatales, to publishing bias meaning agents stories are often inaccurate in order to sell more copies. Further, not only do agents resent these inaccuracies, but they also then have to deal with trying to tell the truth not just for them, but for their fellow agents who cannot, making agents legacies a difficult balancing act in the world of media.

Another layer to these legacies, outside of the media, were the awards agents were given.

Awards



Awards are another unique aspect of gender’s impact on legacy. While female agents were awarded for their time in the field, from the beginning, they were still treated as civilians.

To begin, women could be given an M.B.E., or Member of the British Empire, for their work in the field, but it was a civil M.B.E., rather than military, for women were still not allowed to enlist as soldiers, and as such, could not be given the same treatment. Yvonne Cormeau, Pearl Cornioley Witherington, Phylis Latour, Odette Sansom, Peggy Knight, Blanche Charlet, Eileen Nearne, Lise de Baissac, Anne-Marie Walters, Jacqueline Nearne, Paddy O’Sullivan, and Sonya D’Artois were given Civil M.B.E.’s, and both Lise de Baissac and Pearl Cornioley Witherington turned them down.21 They believed their work was militaristic, not civilian, and they would rather have no award than an improper one. Alternatively, male agents, such as Ralph Beauclerk, Kenneth Mackenzie, Jean Henri Coleman and Charles Skepper all received military M.B.E.’s for doing much of the same work.22 A notable exception to this rule is S.O.E. wireless operator Arthur Breen, who was given a Civil M.B.E., which I believe was because he was part of a volunteer reserve, rather than an enlisted soldier.23

Similarly, women such as Pearl Withinerinton Conrioley were nominated for the Military Cross, but as a woman, could not receive one, while male agents like John Hind Farmer, Peter Lake, Roger Landes, George Reginald Starr, and George Millar all received one.24 Instead, female agents, such as Odette Sansom, were given the George Cross, or, like Nancy Wake, the George Medal, both civilian medals.25

Gender can be seen two-fold through the M.B.E. awards, as you might notice that the female list is far longer than the male list, though it is not complete. On one hand, by being given a civilian M.B.E., female S.O.E. agents’ work was understood as civilian, yet it was still seen as extraordinary at the time, meaning they were more likely to receive M.B.E.’s, while men were not, as it was normalized for them to be soldiers. All in all, this becomes a strange contradiction in women being civilians, not soldiers, yet their military work, in defying gender norms, earned them awards. Through the rejection of the Civil M.B.E. by Pearl, she, Yvonne Cormeau, Sonya D’Artois, Phylis Latour and Paddy O’Sullivan were given the Military M.B.E., as they were beginning to be recognized as soldiers, yet other female agents were not.26 Pearl’s fight also saw men receive proper honours as well, as alongside this group, Arthur Breen was also given a Military M.B.E., despite having been a part of a reserve group.27

Pearl’s story with awards does not end there, as she also fought for parachuting wings, an award given to those deployed by parachute. However, you had to jump at least five times to receive wings, as men did four jumps in training, and a fifth into the field, while women were only allowed to do three jumps in training, and one jump into the field.28 When she returned to England after the war, because of this, she was denied her wings, and claimed that “This was [the military’s] way of granting the award to men only.”29 Pearl, however, fought hard against this rule and demanded wings, and 63 years later, at the age of 92, the Royal Air Force finally granted them to her.30

The legacies of S.O.E. agents are more than exaggerated action movies or headlines, but how we are able to remember, honour, and recognize those whose hard work and sacrifice aided in ending the war. These legacies, such as Pearl Withinerington Cornioley fighting for the Military M.B.E. or being finally allowed her parachute wings 63 years later, should not be seen as heartwarming stories, but rather the consistent refusal to recognize women in the military and in positions of power, who deserve equal recognition and awards as their male counterparts. It would take until 2018 for all combat roles to be available to women and until 2007 for the first woman to be granted the Military Cross, yet as these female S.O.E. agents show, this has not kept women from fighting hard for freedom, despite the lack of recognition and proper honours.31

While much of this legacy portion has focused on female agents, the goal of Understanding the Gendered S.O.E. Experience is to equally educate, honour and recognize S.O.E. agents, regardless of gender, and to see how gender uniquely affected agents in the field. I hope that this section shows the importance of this endeavour in providing an accessible way to understand the intricacies in these agents lives, recognize the hard work they all did, and show just how uneven recognition of S.O.E. agents has been for so long.
